# Teaching Persistence to Ten-Year-Olds

## Executive recommendation

Use a **contrast-first, progressive-reveal curriculum**.

Children should not begin with definitions, the full 54-cell grid, or a large unsorted bag of sentences. They should begin with **small, deliberately engineered sets of ordinary sentences in which one structural difference is made visible at a time**. Only after they can hear and argue about a distinction should the formal term be attached to it.

The learning path should be:

**notice → compare → argue → discriminate → name → navigate → map → retrieve → transfer**

This preserves the brief's intended movement from experience toward formalization, rather than definition-first instruction.

The complete map should eventually become familiar as **six 3×3 neighbourhoods**: one neighbourhood for each Transformation Pattern, with Completion Topology and Persistence Mode giving the two axes inside each neighbourhood. The coordinate is used as an address for navigation, but every completed cell is repeatedly treated as a **single whole structural meaning**, not as three detachable properties.

A crucial qualification: the supplied brief names the six Transformation Patterns but does not provide their full formal semantic definitions. The formulations below are therefore **pedagogical working formulations**, not proposed revisions of the theory. Before classroom release, each must be checked against the relevant *Pixels of Clarity* families and the underlying theoretical specification. The brief explicitly requires ambiguity in the supplied system to be identified rather than silently repaired.

---

# 1. Recommended Pedagogical Strategy

The central principle is:

> **Make children feel the difference before asking them to remember the name of the difference.**

A ten-year-old should first have an experience such as:

> “These two sound almost the same, but something important is different.”

That moment is the instructional engine.

The original idea of sentence strips is therefore good, but the **large unsorted bag should not be the first activity**. A large pile contains too many simultaneously varying features: topic, vocabulary, syntax, transformation, topology and persistence. Children can successfully sort it using irrelevant features—“these mention fixing,” “these sound happy,” “these contain now”—without encountering the intended structure.

Instead, begin with **curated micro-sets of 3–6 sentences**.

Each micro-set should have a pedagogical purpose:

* some sentences are deliberately very similar;
* the important structural variable is controlled;
* irrelevant wording is varied enough to prevent pure keyword matching;
* children must decide which distinction matters;
* the teacher asks for evidence rather than announcing the answer.

Only later should children face messy piles.

The large corpus then becomes extremely valuable: not as material to memorize, but as a **reservoir of retrieval examples, near-neighbours, ambiguous cases and transfer tests**. This fits the brief's advantage that roughly 1,300 ordinary utterances already exist in families rather than requiring artificial textbook examples.

### The governing instructional rules

1. **Contrast before definition.**
2. **One new distinction at a time.**
3. **Names follow successful noticing.**
4. **Every answer requires structural evidence.**
5. **Near-neighbour discrimination matters more than easy categorization.**
6. **Examples encountered during teaching and examples used for assessment must be substantially different.**
7. **The three questions are navigation aids, never an explanation that three meanings are mechanically added together.**
8. **Ambiguity becomes advanced material rather than being disguised as certainty.**

# 2. The Learning Sequence

## Stage 1 — Something is different here

### What the child sees or does

Give pairs or trios of ordinary sentences that seem very similar.

For example:

* “That part is fixed now.”
* “This part is right because it fits the guide.”
* “Fixing that part makes it work again.”

These are especially useful because the brief already identifies them as structurally neighbouring Repair cases.

Ask:

> “These are all about getting something right. Are they saying exactly the same kind of finished thing?”

Children discuss differences.

### What the teacher does

The teacher does **not** supply terminology.

The teacher repeatedly redirects discussion from topic to structure:

> “What remains true at the end?”

> “If I changed the nouns, would your reason still work?”

> “What is the smallest difference between these two?”

### Distinction being learned

There can be structurally important differences beneath very similar subject matter.

### Do not reveal yet

* 54 cells
* coordinates
* dimension names
* formal terms
* the three-question procedure

### Move on when

Most children can give at least one explanation based on the *kind of completed result*, rather than surface vocabulary.

---

## Stage 2 — Find the large families: “What changed?”

### What the child does

Use approximately **12 highly prototypical cards**, initially representing two examples from each of the six Transformation Patterns.

Children sort them into families without labels.

The cards should be selected so the dominant difference really is the character of the transformation.

### Teacher role

Ask children to invent temporary names:

* “making two things different”
* “making a way possible”
* “connecting things”
* “putting something right”
* “making parts into one”
* “bringing something to rest”

Children's exact language is not important.

The teacher asks:

> “What happened in all the cards in this pile?”

> “Could that description also fit the next pile?”

> “What changed from before to after?”

### Distinction learned

The six recurring **Transformation Patterns**.

### Do not reveal yet

Do not show the 54-cell board.

Do not require the six formal names immediately.

### Move on when

A child can sort strong unseen examples into the six provisional families at roughly **80% accuracy** and explain at least four without relying on keywords.

At that point, attach the formal names:

**Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, Settlement.**

The formal word becomes a handle for a distinction already experienced.

---

## Stage 3 — Discover that one change family still contains different kinds of completion

### Child activity

Now stay inside a single well-understood Transformation Pattern—for example Repair.

Give several examples that all clearly involve Repair but differ in **what makes the repair count as completed**.

Ask children to make three sub-piles.

### Teacher role

The teacher deliberately creates frustration:

> “You are all right that these are Repairs. But that answer is no longer enough. Why aren't they identical?”

Eventually attention should turn toward whether completion is concentrated around:

* one decisive locus;
* several parts considered together;
* the disappearance of a previously governing restriction.

### Distinction learned

**Completion Topology**.

Only after children have formed the three-way distinction does the teacher give the labels:

* **Focal**
* **Distributed**
* **Release**

The child-accessible prompts supplied in the brief are excellent starting points for this distinction.

### Do not reveal yet

Do not present Focal, Distributed and Release as three boxes mechanically attached to every Transformation Pattern.

First let children experience the same distinction in two or three different Transformation families.

### Move on when

Children can recognize the three topologies across at least **two different Transformation Patterns**, showing that they have learned the structure rather than one set of Repair examples.

---

## Stage 4 — Discover that completed results persist differently

### Child activity

Hold Transformation Pattern and, as far as possible, Completion Topology stable.

Present a three-way contrast in which the important question becomes:

> “What exactly is still there afterward?”

Children discover a distinction between:

1. the achieved determination itself remaining;
2. something remaining through its fit/relation/support;
3. something remaining because it now works, enables or affects what can happen.

### Distinction learned

**Persistence Mode**.

Then introduce:

* **Standing**
* **Bearing**
* **Efficacy**

This closely follows the distinctions already supplied in the brief.

### Move on when

Children can justify the distinction in unfamiliar examples and can answer:

> “Why Bearing rather than Standing?”

or

> “Why Efficacy rather than Bearing?”

---

## Stage 5 — The three questions become necessary

At this point children have encountered all three kinds of distinction separately.

Now give a mixed set.

Their earlier sorting methods are no longer sufficient.

The teacher says:

> “We need a reliable way to find our way through all these possibilities.”

The class reconstructs three questions from the work it has already done:

1. **What kind of change happened?**
2. **What made the change count as complete?**
3. **How does the finished result remain available?**

The questions are now experienced as **tools invented to solve a real classification problem**, not arbitrary facts handed down by the teacher.

---

## Stage 6 — Reveal the architecture

Only now reveal:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54 positions.**

This is the structure stated in the brief.

Show six 3×3 boards, one for each Transformation Pattern.

Each board has:

* one axis for Completion Topology;
* one axis for Persistence Mode.

Children suddenly see that the distinctions they have independently learned generate the complete navigational architecture.

The teacher should explicitly say:

> “The address helps us find a cell. But the meaning inside the cell is one whole pattern. We are not gluing three separate meanings together.”

That sentence should become a recurring safeguard.

---

## Stage 7 — Neighbour battles

Now instruction shifts from finding obvious cells to discriminating neighbouring ones.

Children receive:

* one sentence;
* its most plausible cell;
* two or three nearby rivals.

They must answer:

> “Why this one rather than that neighbour?”

This is the main route from familiarity to fluency.

---

## Stage 8 — Unseen language and generated examples

Children classify new corpus sentences they have never seen.

Then they generate:

* an example for a named cell;
* a near-miss for the same cell;
* an explanation of what would have to change to move it into a neighbouring cell.

This is the strongest evidence that the structure has become productive knowledge rather than recognition memory.

---

# 3. Discovery of the Three Questions

The questions should not be taught in a single explanatory lesson.

They should emerge because children repeatedly encounter three different kinds of classification failure.

## How Question 1 emerges

Children initially group by topics.

The teacher supplies cards with completely different topics but the same structural transformation, and cards with similar vocabulary but different transformations.

The children's surface sorting breaks.

The teacher asks:

> “Instead of asking what the sentence is *about*, could we ask what happened?”

Eventually:

> **What kind of change happened?**

becomes useful.

Its function is to shrink the search space from 54 to 9 positions, as specified in the brief.

---

## How Question 2 emerges

Children correctly say, for example:

> “These are all Repairs.”

But they encounter several Repairs that clearly do not express the same completed structure.

The teacher asks:

> “Okay. They changed in the same general way. What different thing made each one *finished*?”

This produces:

> **What made it count as complete?**

Children discover Focal, Distributed and Release because a one-dimensional answer has ceased to work.

---

## How Question 3 emerges

Children now have pairs sharing both Transformation Pattern and Completion Topology.

Yet they still detect a difference.

Ask:

> “Suppose the event is over. What does the achievement leave behind?”

Then:

> **How does the finished result remain?**

becomes necessary.

---

## Teaching the navigation procedure

Once discovered, give children three small prompt cards.

**CHANGE**

What kind of change happened?

**COMPLETE**

What made it finished?

**REMAIN**

How is the finished result still available?

Initially children physically place a finger on each prompt.

Later remove the prompts.

But regularly remind them:

> “This is a finding route, not a recipe for constructing the meaning.”

---

# 4. Teaching the Fifteen Definitions

The apparent count of fifteen is best understood as:

* the **three dimension names**;
* their **twelve values**.

That gives fifteen named conceptual items in all.

## Three dimensions

### Transformation Pattern

**Child formulation:**
**What kind of before-to-after change is this?**

This dimension identifies the overall form of transformation.

### Completion Topology

**Child formulation:**
**Where does the reason that it is finished come from?**

Not physical location. It means the *shape of completion*: one decisive locus, completion across multiple elements, or completion through release from something governing the situation.

### Persistence Mode

**Child formulation:**
**What sort of finished achievement is still available afterward?**

This asks how the achieved intelligibility persists.

---

## Six Transformation Patterns

Because the supplied brief gives names but not full formal semantic definitions, these should be treated as **working classroom formulations pending corpus validation**.

### Differentiation

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled by a difference being made clear: **this rather than that, one kind rather than another**.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did becoming distinct do the important work?”

### Opening

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled because **a possibility, route, access or room for action becomes available**.

The brief's example “There is a way through here now” strongly supports this family of imagery.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did something become possible or accessible that wasn't before?”

### Relating

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled because **things are put into a meaningful relation to one another**.

Diagnostic question:

> “Is the important achievement about how things stand in relation?”

### Repair

**Working child formulation:**
Something that was wrong, broken, misaligned or failing becomes **put right enough for the relevant problem to be resolved**.

The supplied corpus examples explicitly include fixing language.

Diagnostic question:

> “Was something wrong, and is its being put right the important change?”

### Integration

**Working child formulation:**
Several pieces become intelligible as **one connected whole**.

The example “It all hangs together” is a useful prototype.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did separate parts become one coherent whole?”

### Settlement

**Working child formulation:**
Something unsettled becomes **resolved, concluded or no longer open in the relevant way**.

“We're square now” is a useful candidate prototype from the supplied material.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did the important change make something finally settled?”

Again, these six formulations require theoretical/corpus validation before being treated as canonical definitions.

---

## Three Completion Topologies

These are more directly supported by the supplied brief.

### Focal

**Child formulation:**
The completion turns on **one decisive point, part, item, place or determination**.

> “This is finished because *that* decisive thing is settled.”

### Distributed

**Child formulation:**
No single part is enough. It becomes complete **across several parts, cases or steps together**.

> “It is finished because the pieces together make it finished.”

### Release

**Child formulation:**
It becomes complete because **something that used to constrain or govern the situation no longer does**.

The brief itself describes Release in this way.

Important distinction:

Release does not merely mean “something stopped.” What matters is that the **ending of its governing force constitutes the completion**.

---

## Three Persistence Modes

### Standing

**Child formulation:**
The achieved determination **itself stands**.

> “It remains settled as what it now is.”

### Bearing

**Child formulation:**
The achievement holds **through how something relates, fits, compares, depends on or is supported by something else**.

> “It remains true because of how it stands with something.”

### Efficacy

**Child formulation:**
The achievement remains as **something that now works, enables, governs, causes or changes what can happen next**.

> “You can tell it remains because it does something.”

These formulations closely track the brief's own child-accessible descriptions.

---

# 5. Revealing the 54-Cell Map

The complete map should appear as a **revelation of structure already learned**, not as page one of the curriculum.

## Visual design

Use **six separate 3×3 panels**.

Each panel represents one Transformation Pattern.

Inside each panel:

**Rows:** Focal / Distributed / Release
**Columns:** Standing / Bearing / Efficacy

or vice versa, provided the orientation never changes.

Children learn:

> “Choose the neighbourhood, then locate the address.”

But every cell should contain its own small **whole-cell descriptor or prototype sentence**, because a coordinate alone risks implying mechanical combination.

### The reveal moment

Place six previously used family headers on the table.

Under one, arrange nine blank slots.

Ask:

> “If every kind of change can finish in these three ways, and every finished result can remain in these three ways, how many different addresses might this neighbourhood contain?”

Children derive 3×3 = 9.

Then:

> “And we have six neighbourhoods.”

Children derive:

**54**.

Now reveal the complete board.

This creates a much stronger mental model than announcing “there are 54 cells” at the beginning.

---

# 6. Sentence-Sorting Game Design

## Phase A: Contrast table

Begin with **6 cards**.

Three form one structural contrast; three provide a second version of the same distinction in different language.

Children work in pairs.

Task:

> Pair each card from Set A with the card from Set B that works in the same structural way.

This is better than free sorting at first because it tightly directs comparison.

---

## Phase B: Six-family sort

Use **12 cards**:

* two strong examples per Transformation Pattern.

Children create six piles.

Temporary child labels are allowed before formal terminology.

---

## Phase C: Nine-box board

Once one Transformation Pattern is secure, give children its 3×3 board.

Start with approximately **5 cards**, not all nine.

Add cards gradually as distinctions become stable.

Eventually fill all nine positions.

---

## Phase D: Mixed-neighbour challenge

Give:

* one target sentence;
* three candidate cells.

At least two candidates must be neighbours.

Points are awarded only when placement **and reason** are correct.

Suggested scoring:

* **1 point:** correct location;
* **1 point:** structural evidence;
* **1 point:** correctly rejecting the nearest rival.

Thus guessing the right square earns little.

---

## Phase E: Change-one-thing

Give a sentence in one cell.

Challenge:

> “Change as few words as possible so it moves exactly one square.”

This is exceptionally useful because the child must manipulate one structural dimension while trying to preserve the others.

---

## Phase F: Build a trap

A child writes an example plus a plausible neighbour.

Another child must detect which structural detail distinguishes them.

Generating convincing traps is advanced mastery.

---

## Preventing keyword matching

Use four systematic safeguards.

### 1. Cross-vocabulary examples

The same structural cell must appear in school, friendship, games, building, everyday objects, rules and stories.

### 2. Keyword reversals

Include two sentences containing the same salient word but belonging in different cells.

### 3. Paraphrase tests

Ask:

> “If I replaced *fixed* with *sorted out*, would your answer change?”

### 4. Reason-before-reveal

A placement is never treated as secure until the child completes:

> “I put it here because…”

The goal is exactly the brief's requirement that children classify using **structural evidence**, not merely surface resemblance.

---

# 7. Teacher Discussion Guide

The teacher's most important move is:

> **Ask for the discriminating evidence.**

Useful recurring questions:

* “What changed?”
* “What exactly made it finished?”
* “What remains true afterward?”
* “What part of the sentence is your evidence?”
* “Which neighbour almost works?”
* “Why doesn't that neighbour quite work?”
* “Would your rule still work if we changed the topic?”
* “What is the smallest change that would make you move it?”
* “Can someone argue for the other square?”
* “Are we disagreeing about the structure, or is the sentence itself genuinely underspecified?”

## Correct placement, weak reasoning

Do not say merely “Correct.”

Say:

> “You found the right place. Now convince someone who chose the square next door.”

Correct guessing must not masquerade as mastery.

---

## Incorrect placement, interesting reasoning

Preserve the reasoning.

Say:

> “Your reason notices something real. Let's test whether it is the feature that actually decides this distinction.”

Then compare the target with a minimal neighbour.

The child should discover exactly where the reasoning stops discriminating.

---

## Two plausible neighbouring placements

Ask each side to identify:

1. evidence supporting its cell;
2. evidence against the rival;
3. what extra information would settle the case.

This transforms disagreement from voting into model comparison.

---

## Genuinely ambiguous example

Mark it **AMBIGUOUS**, rather than forcing consensus.

Ask:

> “What reading puts it here?”

> “What different reading puts it there?”

> “What extra words would remove the ambiguity?”

This is especially important because the supplied corpus may contain genuine interpretive instability, which the brief explicitly says should not automatically be tidied away.

Ambiguity should therefore become an advanced reasoning task.

---

# 8. Contrast Sets

The strongest curriculum resource should be a library of **minimal or near-minimal contrasts**.

## Set A — Persistence contrast inside Repair

Use the supplied trio:

> “That part is fixed now.”

> “This part is right because it fits the guide.”

> “Fixing that part makes it work again.”

The pedagogical target is the difference between a repaired determination that stands, a repaired condition understood through fit/relation, and a repair expressed through restored functioning.

This is the model contrast set for teaching **Standing / Bearing / Efficacy**.

Exact cell assignment should be validated against the corpus annotations before classroom use; the pedagogical value is the tightly controlled contrast.

---

## Set B — Same vocabulary, different structural work

Synthetic prototype, to be replaced or validated using corpus items:

> “The gate is open now.”

> “The gate is open because both catches line up.”

> “Opening the gate lets everyone through.”

The word *open* appears repeatedly, but children must decide whether the structural evidence concerns achieved state, relational condition or efficacy.

Purpose:

**destroy the strategy “spot a word, choose a box.”**

---

## Set C — Focal / Distributed / Release

Synthetic prototype:

> “Getting this final piece into place completes the picture.”

> “Only when all six pieces are in place is the picture complete.”

> “Once the rule requiring matching pieces is removed, the design is allowed to count as complete.”

The language should ultimately be replaced by natural *Pixels of Clarity* items sharing a Transformation Pattern as tightly as possible.

Purpose:

make the topology distinction visible:

* decisive locus;
* collective completion;
* completion through ceased constraint.

---

## Set D — Transformation contrast while topology and persistence are held as stable as possible

Synthetic prototype:

> “Now the red one is clearly different from the orange one.”
> **Differentiation**

> “Now there is a way into the garden.”
> **Opening**

> “Now the clue matches the map.”
> **Relating**

> “Now the broken latch is right again.”
> **Repair**

> “Now all the clues form one story.”
> **Integration**

> “Now the argument is settled.”
> **Settlement**

Purpose:

teach **what changed?** while minimizing unnecessary linguistic complexity.

The important production task for V1 is to locate better corpus-backed versions of precisely this kind.

---

## Set E — One-square movement

Start with a secure example.

Then show a minimally altered sentence intended to change only one coordinate.

Ask:

> “Did we move to another neighbourhood, another row, or another column?”

This directly builds the child's mental geometry of the 54-cell system.

---

# 9. Assessment and Transfer

Assessment should measure **productive structural reasoning**, not familiarity with training cards.

Use a compact six-part assessment.

## A. Dimension reconstruction

Without seeing the reference card, the child explains the three questions in their own words.

Success means the child distinguishes:

* kind of transformation;
* source/shape of completion;
* mode of persistence.

Exact wording is unnecessary.

---

## B. Fifteen-term match

Give the 15 names and short situations.

The child matches and explains them.

For the 12 values, assessment should include near-neighbours rather than only obvious prototypes.

---

## C. Guided navigation

Present four unseen sentences.

For each, the child travels through the three questions and proposes a cell.

Score the reasoning at each discrimination.

---

## D. Neighbour exclusion

For three items provide:

* best cell;
* one near neighbour.

Ask:

> “Why not the neighbour?”

This is more diagnostic than asking only for the correct answer.

---

## E. Far transfer

Use previously unseen sentences whose subjects and vocabulary differ substantially from all instructional examples.

No sentence from the teaching deck should appear here.

This measures whether the child has abstracted structure from wording.

---

## F. Generation

Give a coordinate, for example:

**Repair × Focal × Efficacy**

Ask the child to invent:

1. an example belonging there;
2. a near-miss belonging in an adjacent cell;
3. the structural difference between them.

Generation is the strongest compact test.

---

## Suggested mastery standard

A child is fluent enough for the stated goal when they can:

* explain all three dimensions;
* identify the 12 dimension values with high reliability;
* classify approximately **80–85% of clear unseen cases**;
* justify the majority using genuine structural evidence;
* reject a plausible neighbouring cell;
* generate at least several valid examples from specified coordinates.

Do **not** require perfect classification of genuinely ambiguous corpus items.

---

# 10. Recommended Duration

The shortest credible initial route is approximately **six 45-minute sessions**, followed by two very short spaced retrieval sessions.

## Session 1 — Notice and Transformation Patterns

Contrastive examples, temporary families, emergence of “What changed?”

Introduce the six Transformation labels late in the session.

## Session 2 — Completion Topology

Work inside known Transformation families.

Discover Focal / Distributed / Release.

## Session 3 — Persistence Mode

Minimal contrasts.

Discover Standing / Bearing / Efficacy.

## Session 4 — Three-question navigation + 54-map reveal

Children reconstruct the three questions and then see the full architecture.

Begin nine-box work.

## Session 5 — Neighbour battles

Mixed classification, one-square moves, reasons and rival exclusions.

## Session 6 — Transfer and production

Unseen sentences, ambiguous cases, generation and compact assessment.

Then add:

* **10-minute retrieval after 2–3 days**
* **10-minute retrieval about a week later**

If performance is weak, extend practice rather than adding more explanation.

Approximately **4.5 hours of direct instruction plus 20 minutes of spaced retrieval** is a credible minimum prototype target.

Trying to teach the system substantially faster risks producing terminology recognition without usable discrimination.

# 11. Risks and Failure Modes

## Revealing 54 boxes immediately

The board becomes a memorization task before the child understands what any boundary means.

**Remedy:** reveal the architecture after the dimensions have become useful.

---

## Fifteen definitions before experience

Children memorize verbal paraphrases while lacking discriminable concepts.

**Remedy:** names follow contrast.

---

## Beginning with the entire sentence bag

Children invent uncontrolled sorting principles.

**Remedy:** curated micro-sets first; messy sorting later.

---

## Prototype-only teaching

Children recognize only extremely obvious examples.

**Remedy:** move quickly from prototypes to near-neighbours.

---

## Keyword classification

“Fixed means Repair.”
“Works means Efficacy.”

This can produce high apparent scores with no structural understanding.

**Remedy:** lexical traps, paraphrases, cross-domain examples and mandatory explanation.

---

## Treating the three questions as an assembly algorithm

Children may conclude that a cell means simply:

**Transformation + Topology + Persistence**

as three independently attached features.

That contradicts the supplied system, where the dimensions mutually constrain the unified cell meaning.

**Remedy:** call the questions an **address-finding procedure**, then repeatedly study whole-cell families.

---

## Confusing a coordinate with understanding a cell

Knowing “Repair × Focal × Bearing” is not enough.

**Remedy:** every cell needs prototype examples, rivals and explanation of its characteristic unified form.

---

## Too many cards

Volume can masquerade as rigor while overwhelming comparison.

**Remedy:** fewer, carefully selected contrasts.

---

## Badly chosen minimal pairs

A supposed one-dimensional contrast may secretly differ in two or three dimensions.

**Remedy:** expert validation of every anchor contrast before classroom use.

---

## Hiding all ambiguity

Children learn a false model in which language is always uniquely determined.

**Remedy:** use clear cases first, then carefully introduce ambiguity.

---

## Introducing ambiguity too early

Before boundaries are stable, ambiguity simply looks like arbitrariness.

**Remedy:** ambiguous cases belong after initial map competence.

---

## Unequal corpus quality across cells

Some positions may have many clear exemplars while others contain unstable families.

This is a property requiring empirical audit, not something curriculum design should conceal.

**Remedy:** give every cell a confidence status during corpus preparation:

* anchor-ready;
* usable;
* advanced/ambiguous;
* unresolved.

---

## Assessing with memorized training sentences

This measures recall of examples rather than structural transfer.

**Remedy:** reserve unseen corpus material for assessment.

---

## Teacher over-explanation

A teacher can unintentionally do all the discrimination for the child.

**Remedy:** ask contrast questions before explaining.

---

## False precision in the six Transformation definitions

The present brief does not supply sufficient formal definitions to guarantee that simplified classroom glosses preserve every intended boundary.

**Remedy:** perform a dedicated corpus/theory validation pass before freezing the teacher language.

This is probably the most important unresolved content-design issue before V1.

---

# 12. Final Recommended Prototype

Version 1 should be deliberately small.

Do **not** begin by building software or converting all 1,300 utterances into cards.

Build one flat repository/document and a small printable deck.

## A. Core repository document

Create these sections:

### 1. Theory reference

One page containing:

* three dimensions;
* 12 dimension values;
* 6×3×3 structure;
* explicit warning that coordinates constrain a unified cell rather than mechanically composing it.

### 2. Teacher's three questions

One page containing:

* What kind of change happened?
* What made it count as complete?
* How does the finished result remain?

Plus diagnostic prompts underneath each.

### 3. Fifteen-term teaching glossary

Use the formulations above only after they have been validated against the corpus and formal theory.

### 4. Six 3×3 maps

One map per Transformation Pattern.

Each cell should eventually contain:

* coordinate;
* one short whole-cell gloss;
* one anchor example;
* one nearest rival;
* one sentence explaining the distinction.

### 5. Contrast-set library

Prioritize approximately **18–24 highly controlled contrast sets** rather than hundreds of individual examples.

Each set records:

* target distinction;
* dimensions held stable;
* examples;
* expected classification;
* nearest rival;
* discriminating evidence;
* ambiguity notes.

### 6. Teaching script

The six-session sequence in this document.

### 7. Assessment pack

Keep all assessment examples separate from teaching examples.

### 8. Corpus-confidence notes

Flag structurally unstable cells instead of forcing them into clean instructional stories.

---

## B. Printable Version 1 card deck

Start with approximately **72 cards**, not 1,300.

A useful distribution is:

* **24 anchor cards** — very clear prototypes;
* **24 contrast cards** — chosen to create near-neighbour comparisons;
* **12 lexical-trap cards** — similar wording, different structures;
* **12 transfer cards** — held back from instruction.

The numbers can change after pilot testing. The design principle matters more than exact arithmetic.

Every cell does **not** need equal representation in the first teaching deck if some cells lack sufficiently clean examples.

A weak example should never be included merely to achieve numerical symmetry.

---

## C. The first corpus-selection task

Before classroom production, create a table for all 54 cells and ask expert annotators to nominate:

**Best prototype**
The clearest ordinary example.

**Best neighbour contrast**
An example that makes one local boundary visible.

**Best paraphrase pair**
Different wording, same structure.

**Best lexical trap**
Similar wording, different structure.

**Ambiguity candidate**
A case worth discussing later.

This turns *Pixels of Clarity* from a large archive into an instructional resource.

The existing corpus is particularly valuable because it contains convergence, variation and potentially informative disagreement.

---

## D. What to build first

The first tangible prototype should therefore be:

**1 teacher guide + 6 printable 3×3 boards + approximately 72 sentence cards + 1 short unseen assessment.**

Do not build the digital interface yet.

Run the physical version first.

Watch for five things:

1. Where children spontaneously invent the intended distinctions.
2. Which formal names they can attach easily after discovery.
3. Where they revert to keyword matching.
4. Which neighbouring cells repeatedly collapse together.
5. Which corpus items produce principled disagreement.

Those observations should determine the design of the eventual computer interface.

The computer should solve a problem children have already experienced—keeping track of a rich 54-position space—rather than introducing complexity before they know why the map exists. This preserves the strongest feature of the initial physical-to-digital idea in the brief.

---

# Final design principle

A child should never be asked to memorize a distinction that could first be **made visible through a comparison**.

The 54-cell architecture should arrive as the satisfying answer to a problem the child now understands:

> “We keep finding the same kinds of difference inside different kinds of completed events. How can we keep track of all of them?”

At that point the grid is not an arbitrary taxonomy.

It is a map of distinctions the child has already learned to see.

That is the shortest plausible route from ordinary sentences to the outcome the brief specifies: a child who can recognize a structure, locate it, defend the placement against its neighbours and transfer the distinction to new language.
