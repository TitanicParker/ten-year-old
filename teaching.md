# Teaching Persistence to Ten-Year-Olds

## Executive recommendation

Use a **contrast-first, progressive-reveal curriculum**.

Children should not begin with definitions, the full 54-cell grid, or a large unsorted bag of sentences. They should begin with **small, deliberately engineered sets of ordinary sentences in which one structural difference is made visible at a time**. Only after they can hear and argue about a distinction should the formal term be attached to it.

The learning path should be:

**notice → compare → argue → discriminate → name → navigate → map → retrieve → transfer**

This preserves the brief's intended movement from experience toward formalization, rather than definition-first instruction.

The complete map should eventually become familiar as **six 3×3 neighbourhoods**: one neighbourhood for each Transformation Pattern, with Completion Topology and Persistence Mode giving the two axes inside each neighbourhood. The coordinate is used as an address for navigation, but every completed cell is repeatedly treated as a **single whole structural meaning**, not as three detachable properties.

A crucial qualification: the supplied brief names the six Transformation Patterns but does not provide their full formal semantic definitions. The formulations below are therefore **pedagogical working formulations**, not proposed revisions of the theory. Before classroom release, each must be checked against the relevant *Pixels of Clarity* families and the underlying theoretical specification. The brief explicitly requires ambiguity in the supplied system to be identified rather than silently repaired.

---

# 1. Recommended Pedagogical Strategy

The central principle is:

> **Make children feel the difference before asking them to remember the name of the difference.**

A ten-year-old should first have an experience such as:

> “These two sound almost the same, but something important is different.”

That moment is the instructional engine.

The original idea of sentence strips is therefore good, but the **large unsorted bag should not be the first activity**. A large pile contains too many simultaneously varying features: topic, vocabulary, syntax, transformation, topology and persistence. Children can successfully sort it using irrelevant features—“these mention fixing,” “these sound happy,” “these contain now”—without encountering the intended structure.

Instead, begin with **curated micro-sets of 3–6 sentences**.

Each micro-set should have a pedagogical purpose:

* some sentences are deliberately very similar;
* the important structural variable is controlled;
* irrelevant wording is varied enough to prevent pure keyword matching;
* children must decide which distinction matters;
* the teacher asks for evidence rather than announcing the answer.

Only later should children face messy piles.

The large corpus then becomes extremely valuable: not as material to memorize, but as a **reservoir of retrieval examples, near-neighbours, ambiguous cases and transfer tests**. This fits the brief's advantage that roughly 1,300 ordinary utterances already exist in families rather than requiring artificial textbook examples.

### The governing instructional rules

1. **Contrast before definition.**
2. **One new distinction at a time.**
3. **Names follow successful noticing.**
4. **Every answer requires structural evidence.**
5. **Near-neighbour discrimination matters more than easy categorization.**
6. **Examples encountered during teaching and examples used for assessment must be substantially different.**
7. **The three questions are navigation aids, never an explanation that three meanings are mechanically added together.**
8. **Ambiguity becomes advanced material rather than being disguised as certainty.**

# 2. The Learning Sequence

## Stage 1 — Something is different here

### What the child sees or does

Give pairs or trios of ordinary sentences that seem very similar.

For example:

* “That part is fixed now.”
* “This part is right because it fits the guide.”
* “Fixing that part makes it work again.”

These are especially useful because the brief already identifies them as structurally neighbouring Repair cases.

Ask:

> “These are all about getting something right. Are they saying exactly the same kind of finished thing?”

Children discuss differences.

### What the teacher does

The teacher does **not** supply terminology.

The teacher repeatedly redirects discussion from topic to structure:

> “What remains true at the end?”

> “If I changed the nouns, would your reason still work?”

> “What is the smallest difference between these two?”

### Distinction being learned

There can be structurally important differences beneath very similar subject matter.

### Do not reveal yet

* 54 cells
* coordinates
* dimension names
* formal terms
* the three-question procedure

### Move on when

Most children can give at least one explanation based on the *kind of completed result*, rather than surface vocabulary.

---

## Stage 2 — Find the large families: “What changed?”

### What the child does

Use approximately **12 highly prototypical cards**, initially representing two examples from each of the six Transformation Patterns.

Children sort them into families without labels.

The cards should be selected so the dominant difference really is the character of the transformation.

### Teacher role

Ask children to invent temporary names:

* “making two things different”
* “making a way possible”
* “connecting things”
* “putting something right”
* “making parts into one”
* “bringing something to rest”

Children's exact language is not important.

The teacher asks:

> “What happened in all the cards in this pile?”

> “Could that description also fit the next pile?”

> “What changed from before to after?”

### Distinction learned

The six recurring **Transformation Patterns**.

### Do not reveal yet

Do not show the 54-cell board.

Do not require the six formal names immediately.

### Move on when

A child can sort strong unseen examples into the six provisional families at roughly **80% accuracy** and explain at least four without relying on keywords.

At that point, attach the formal names:

**Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, Settlement.**

The formal word becomes a handle for a distinction already experienced.

---

## Stage 3 — Discover that one change family still contains different kinds of completion

### Child activity

Now stay inside a single well-understood Transformation Pattern—for example Repair.

Give several examples that all clearly involve Repair but differ in **what makes the repair count as completed**.

Ask children to make three sub-piles.

### Teacher role

The teacher deliberately creates frustration:

> “You are all right that these are Repairs. But that answer is no longer enough. Why aren't they identical?”

Eventually attention should turn toward whether completion is concentrated around:

* one decisive locus;
* several parts considered together;
* the disappearance of a previously governing restriction.

### Distinction learned

**Completion Topology**.

Only after children have formed the three-way distinction does the teacher give the labels:

* **Focal**
* **Distributed**
* **Release**

The child-accessible prompts supplied in the brief are excellent starting points for this distinction.

### Do not reveal yet

Do not present Focal, Distributed and Release as three boxes mechanically attached to every Transformation Pattern.

First let children experience the same distinction in two or three different Transformation families.

### Move on when

Children can recognize the three topologies across at least **two different Transformation Patterns**, showing that they have learned the structure rather than one set of Repair examples.

---

## Stage 4 — Discover that completed results persist differently

### Child activity

Hold Transformation Pattern and, as far as possible, Completion Topology stable.

Present a three-way contrast in which the important question becomes:

> “What exactly is still there afterward?”

Children discover a distinction between:

1. the achieved determination itself remaining;
2. something remaining through its fit/relation/support;
3. something remaining because it now works, enables or affects what can happen.

### Distinction learned

**Persistence Mode**.

Then introduce:

* **Standing**
* **Bearing**
* **Efficacy**

This closely follows the distinctions already supplied in the brief.

### Move on when

Children can justify the distinction in unfamiliar examples and can answer:

> “Why Bearing rather than Standing?”

or

> “Why Efficacy rather than Bearing?”

---

## Stage 5 — The three questions become necessary

At this point children have encountered all three kinds of distinction separately.

Now give a mixed set.

Their earlier sorting methods are no longer sufficient.

The teacher says:

> “We need a reliable way to find our way through all these possibilities.”

The class reconstructs three questions from the work it has already done:

1. **What kind of change happened?**
2. **What made the change count as complete?**
3. **How does the finished result remain available?**

The questions are now experienced as **tools invented to solve a real classification problem**, not arbitrary facts handed down by the teacher.

---

## Stage 6 — Reveal the architecture

Only now reveal:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54 positions.**

This is the structure stated in the brief.

Show six 3×3 boards, one for each Transformation Pattern.

Each board has:

* one axis for Completion Topology;
* one axis for Persistence Mode.

Children suddenly see that the distinctions they have independently learned generate the complete navigational architecture.

The teacher should explicitly say:

> “The address helps us find a cell. But the meaning inside the cell is one whole pattern. We are not gluing three separate meanings together.”

That sentence should become a recurring safeguard.

---

## Stage 7 — Neighbour battles

Now instruction shifts from finding obvious cells to discriminating neighbouring ones.

Children receive:

* one sentence;
* its most plausible cell;
* two or three nearby rivals.

They must answer:

> “Why this one rather than that neighbour?”

This is the main route from familiarity to fluency.

---

## Stage 8 — Unseen language and generated examples

Children classify new corpus sentences they have never seen.

Then they generate:

* an example for a named cell;
* a near-miss for the same cell;
* an explanation of what would have to change to move it into a neighbouring cell.

This is the strongest evidence that the structure has become productive knowledge rather than recognition memory.

---

# 3. Discovery of the Three Questions

The questions should not be taught in a single explanatory lesson.

They should emerge because children repeatedly encounter three different kinds of classification failure.

## How Question 1 emerges

Children initially group by topics.

The teacher supplies cards with completely different topics but the same structural transformation, and cards with similar vocabulary but different transformations.

The children's surface sorting breaks.

The teacher asks:

> “Instead of asking what the sentence is *about*, could we ask what happened?”

Eventually:

> **What kind of change happened?**

becomes useful.

Its function is to shrink the search space from 54 to 9 positions, as specified in the brief.

---

## How Question 2 emerges

Children correctly say, for example:

> “These are all Repairs.”

But they encounter several Repairs that clearly do not express the same completed structure.

The teacher asks:

> “Okay. They changed in the same general way. What different thing made each one *finished*?”

This produces:

> **What made it count as complete?**

Children discover Focal, Distributed and Release because a one-dimensional answer has ceased to work.

---

## How Question 3 emerges

Children now have pairs sharing both Transformation Pattern and Completion Topology.

Yet they still detect a difference.

Ask:

> “Suppose the event is over. What does the achievement leave behind?”

Then:

> **How does the finished result remain?**

becomes necessary.

---

## Teaching the navigation procedure

Once discovered, give children three small prompt cards.

**CHANGE**

What kind of change happened?

**COMPLETE**

What made it finished?

**REMAIN**

How is the finished result still available?

Initially children physically place a finger on each prompt.

Later remove the prompts.

But regularly remind them:

> “This is a finding route, not a recipe for constructing the meaning.”

---

# 4. Teaching the Fifteen Definitions

The apparent count of fifteen is best understood as:

* the **three dimension names**;
* their **twelve values**.

That gives fifteen named conceptual items in all.

## Three dimensions

### Transformation Pattern

**Child formulation:**
**What kind of before-to-after change is this?**

This dimension identifies the overall form of transformation.

### Completion Topology

**Child formulation:**
**Where does the reason that it is finished come from?**

Not physical location. It means the *shape of completion*: one decisive locus, completion across multiple elements, or completion through release from something governing the situation.

### Persistence Mode

**Child formulation:**
**What sort of finished achievement is still available afterward?**

This asks how the achieved intelligibility persists.

---

## Six Transformation Patterns

Because the supplied brief gives names but not full formal semantic definitions, these should be treated as **working classroom formulations pending corpus validation**.

### Differentiation

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled by a difference being made clear: **this rather than that, one kind rather than another**.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did becoming distinct do the important work?”

### Opening

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled because **a possibility, route, access or room for action becomes available**.

The brief's example “There is a way through here now” strongly supports this family of imagery.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did something become possible or accessible that wasn't before?”

### Relating

**Working child formulation:**
Something becomes settled because **things are put into a meaningful relation to one another**.

Diagnostic question:

> “Is the important achievement about how things stand in relation?”

### Repair

**Working child formulation:**
Something that was wrong, broken, misaligned or failing becomes **put right enough for the relevant problem to be resolved**.

The supplied corpus examples explicitly include fixing language.

Diagnostic question:

> “Was something wrong, and is its being put right the important change?”

### Integration

**Working child formulation:**
Several pieces become intelligible as **one connected whole**.

The example “It all hangs together” is a useful prototype.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did separate parts become one coherent whole?”

### Settlement

**Working child formulation:**
Something unsettled becomes **resolved, concluded or no longer open in the relevant way**.

“We're square now” is a useful candidate prototype from the supplied material.

Diagnostic question:

> “Did the important change make something finally settled?”

Again, these six formulations require theoretical/corpus validation before being treated as canonical definitions.

---

## Three Completion Topologies

These are more directly supported by the supplied brief.

### Focal

**Child formulation:**
The completion turns on **one decisive point, part, item, place or determination**.

> “This is finished because *that* decisive thing is settled.”

### Distributed

**Child formulation:**
No single part is enough. It becomes complete **across several parts, cases or steps together**.

> “It is finished because the pieces together make it finished.”

### Release

**Child formulation:**
It becomes complete because **something that used to constrain or govern the situation no longer does**.

The brief itself describes Release in this way.

Important distinction:

Release does not merely mean “something stopped.” What matters is that the **ending of its governing force constitutes the completion**.

---

## Three Persistence Modes

### Standing

**Child formulation:**
The achieved determination **itself stands**.

> “It remains settled as what it now is.”

### Bearing

**Child formulation:**
The achievement holds **through how something relates, fits, compares, depends on or is supported by something else**.

> “It remains true because of how it stands with something.”

### Efficacy

**Child formulation:**
The achievement remains as **something that now works, enables, governs, causes or changes what can happen next**.

> “You can tell it remains because it does something.”

These formulations closely track the brief's own child-accessible descriptions.

---

# 5. Revealing the 54-Cell Map

The complete map should appear as a **revelation of structure already learned**, not as page one of the curriculum.

## Visual design

Use **six separate 3×3 panels**.

Each panel represents one Transformation Pattern.

Inside each panel:

**Rows:** Focal / Distributed / Release
**Columns:** Standing / Bearing / Efficacy

or vice versa, provided the orientation never changes.

Children learn:

> “Choose the neighbourhood, then locate the address.”

But every cell should contain its own small **whole-cell descriptor or prototype sentence**, because a coordinate alone risks implying mechanical combination.

### The reveal moment

Place six previously used family headers on the table.

Under one, arrange nine blank slots.

Ask:

> “If every kind of change can finish in these three ways, and every finished result can remain in these three ways, how many different addresses might this neighbourhood contain?”

Children derive 3×3 = 9.

Then:

> “And we have six neighbourhoods.”

Children derive:

**54**.

Now reveal the complete board.

This creates a much stronger mental model than announcing “there are 54 cells” at the beginning.

---

# 6. Sentence-Sorting Game Design

## Phase A: Contrast table

Begin with **6 cards**.

Three form one structural contrast; three provide a second version of the same distinction in different language.

Children work in pairs.

Task:

> Pair each card from Set A with the card from Set B that works in the same structural way.

This is better than free sorting at first because it tightly directs comparison.

---

## Phase B: Six-family sort

Use **12 cards**:

* two strong examples per Transformation Pattern.

Children create six piles.

Temporary child labels are allowed before formal terminology.

---

## Phase C: Nine-box board

Once one Transformation Pattern is secure, give children its 3×3 board.

Start with approximately **5 cards**, not all nine.

Add cards gradually as distinctions become stable.

Eventually fill all nine positions.

---

## Phase D: Mixed-neighbour challenge

Give:

* one target sentence;
* three candidate cells.

At least two candidates must be neighbours.

Points are awarded only when placement **and reason** are correct.

Suggested scoring:

* **1 point:** correct location;
* **1 point:** structural evidence;
* **1 point:** correctly rejecting the nearest rival.

Thus guessing the right square earns little.

---

## Phase E: Change-one-thing

Give a sentence in one cell.

Challenge:

> “Change as few words as possible so it moves exactly one square.”

This is exceptionally useful because the child must manipulate one structural dimension while trying to preserve the others.

---

## Phase F: Build a trap

A child writes an example plus a plausible neighbour.

Another child must detect which structural detail distinguishes them.

Generating convincing traps is advanced mastery.

---

## Preventing keyword matching

Use four systematic safeguards.

### 1. Cross-vocabulary examples

The same structural cell must appear in school, friendship, games, building, everyday objects, rules and stories.

### 2. Keyword reversals

Include two sentences containing the same salient word but belonging in different cells.

### 3. Paraphrase tests

Ask:

> “If I replaced *fixed* with *sorted out*, would your answer change?”

### 4. Reason-before-reveal

A placement is never treated as secure until the child completes:

> “I put it here because…”

The goal is exactly the brief's requirement that children classify using **structural evidence**, not merely surface resemblance.

---

# 7. Teacher Discussion Guide

The teacher's most important move is:

> **Ask for the discriminating evidence.**

Useful recurring questions:

* “What changed?”
* “What exactly made it finished?”
* “What remains true afterward?”
* “What part of the sentence is your evidence?”
* “Which neighbour almost works?”
* “Why doesn't that neighbour quite work?”
* “Would your rule still work if we changed the topic?”
* “What is the smallest change that would make you move it?”
* “Can someone argue for the other square?”
* “Are we disagreeing about the structure, or is the sentence itself genuinely underspecified?”

## Correct placement, weak reasoning

Do not say merely “Correct.”

Say:

> “You found the right place. Now convince someone who chose the square next door.”

Correct guessing must not masquerade as mastery.

---

## Incorrect placement, interesting reasoning

Preserve the reasoning.

Say:

> “Your reason notices something real. Let's test whether it is the feature that actually decides this distinction.”

Then compare the target with a minimal neighbour.

The child should discover exactly where the reasoning stops discriminating.

---

## Two plausible neighbouring placements

Ask each side to identify:

1. evidence supporting its cell;
2. evidence against the rival;
3. what extra information would settle the case.

This transforms disagreement from voting into model comparison.

---

## Genuinely ambiguous example

Mark it **AMBIGUOUS**, rather than forcing consensus.

Ask:

> “What reading puts it here?”

> “What different reading puts it there?”

> “What extra words would remove the ambiguity?”

This is especially important because the supplied corpus may contain genuine interpretive instability, which the brief explicitly says should not automatically be tidied away.

Ambiguity should therefore become an advanced reasoning task.

---

# 8. Contrast Sets

The strongest curriculum resource should be a library of **minimal or near-minimal contrasts**.

## Set A — Persistence contrast inside Repair

Use the supplied trio:

> “That part is fixed now.”

> “This part is right because it fits the guide.”

> “Fixing that part makes it work again.”

The pedagogical target is the difference between a repaired determination that stands, a repaired condition understood through fit/relation, and a repair expressed through restored functioning.

This is the model contrast set for teaching **Standing / Bearing / Efficacy**.

Exact cell assignment should be validated against the corpus annotations before classroom use; the pedagogical value is the tightly controlled contrast.

---

## Set B — Same vocabulary, different structural work

Synthetic prototype, to be replaced or validated using corpus items:

> “The gate is open now.”

> “The gate is open because both catches line up.”

> “Opening the gate lets everyone through.”

The word *open* appears repeatedly, but children must decide whether the structural evidence concerns achieved state, relational condition or efficacy.

Purpose:

**destroy the strategy “spot a word, choose a box.”**

---

## Set C — Focal / Distributed / Release

Synthetic prototype:

> “Getting this final piece into place completes the picture.”

> “Only when all six pieces are in place is the picture complete.”

> “Once the rule requiring matching pieces is removed, the design is allowed to count as complete.”

The language should ultimately be replaced by natural *Pixels of Clarity* items sharing a Transformation Pattern as tightly as possible.

Purpose:

make the topology distinction visible:

* decisive locus;
* collective completion;
* completion through ceased constraint.

---

## Set D — Transformation contrast while topology and persistence are held as stable as possible

Synthetic prototype:

> “Now the red one is clearly different from the orange one.”
> **Differentiation**

> “Now there is a way into the garden.”
> **Opening**

> “Now the clue matches the map.”
> **Relating**

> “Now the broken latch is right again.”
> **Repair**

> “Now all the clues form one story.”
> **Integration**

> “Now the argument is settled.”
> **Settlement**

Purpose:

teach **what changed?** while minimizing unnecessary linguistic complexity.

The important production task for V1 is to locate better corpus-backed versions of precisely this kind.

---

## Set E — One-square movement

Start with a secure example.

Then show a minimally altered sentence intended to change only one coordinate.

Ask:

> “Did we move to another neighbourhood, another row, or another column?”

This directly builds the child's mental geometry of the 54-cell system.

---

# 9. Assessment and Transfer

Assessment should measure **productive structural reasoning**, not familiarity with training cards.

Use a compact six-part assessment.

## A. Dimension reconstruction

Without seeing the reference card, the child explains the three questions in their own words.

Success means the child distinguishes:

* kind of transformation;
* source/shape of completion;
* mode of persistence.

Exact wording is unnecessary.

---

## B. Fifteen-term match

Give the 15 names and short situations.

The child matches and explains them.

For the 12 values, assessment should include near-neighbours rather than only obvious prototypes.

---

## C. Guided navigation

Present four unseen sentences.

For each, the child travels through the three questions and proposes a cell.

Score the reasoning at each discrimination.

---

## D. Neighbour exclusion

For three items provide:

* best cell;
* one near neighbour.

Ask:

> “Why not the neighbour?”

This is more diagnostic than asking only for the correct answer.

---

## E. Far transfer

Use previously unseen sentences whose subjects and vocabulary differ substantially from all instructional examples.

No sentence from the teaching deck should appear here.

This measures whether the child has abstracted structure from wording.

---

## F. Generation

Give a coordinate, for example:

**Repair × Focal × Efficacy**

Ask the child to invent:

1. an example belonging there;
2. a near-miss belonging in an adjacent cell;
3. the structural difference between them.

Generation is the strongest compact test.

---

## Suggested mastery standard

A child is fluent enough for the stated goal when they can:

* explain all three dimensions;
* identify the 12 dimension values with high reliability;
* classify approximately **80–85% of clear unseen cases**;
* justify the majority using genuine structural evidence;
* reject a plausible neighbouring cell;
* generate at least several valid examples from specified coordinates.

Do **not** require perfect classification of genuinely ambiguous corpus items.

---

# 10. Recommended Duration

The shortest credible initial route is approximately **six 45-minute sessions**, followed by two very short spaced retrieval sessions.

## Session 1 — Notice and Transformation Patterns

Contrastive examples, temporary families, emergence of “What changed?”

Introduce the six Transformation labels late in the session.

## Session 2 — Completion Topology

Work inside known Transformation families.

Discover Focal / Distributed / Release.

## Session 3 — Persistence Mode

Minimal contrasts.

Discover Standing / Bearing / Efficacy.

## Session 4 — Three-question navigation + 54-map reveal

Children reconstruct the three questions and then see the full architecture.

Begin nine-box work.

## Session 5 — Neighbour battles

Mixed classification, one-square moves, reasons and rival exclusions.

## Session 6 — Transfer and production

Unseen sentences, ambiguous cases, generation and compact assessment.

Then add:

* **10-minute retrieval after 2–3 days**
* **10-minute retrieval about a week later**

If performance is weak, extend practice rather than adding more explanation.

Approximately **4.5 hours of direct instruction plus 20 minutes of spaced retrieval** is a credible minimum prototype target.

Trying to teach the system substantially faster risks producing terminology recognition without usable discrimination.

# 11. Risks and Failure Modes

## Revealing 54 boxes immediately

The board becomes a memorization task before the child understands what any boundary means.

**Remedy:** reveal the architecture after the dimensions have become useful.

---

## Fifteen definitions before experience

Children memorize verbal paraphrases while lacking discriminable concepts.

**Remedy:** names follow contrast.

---

## Beginning with the entire sentence bag

Children invent uncontrolled sorting principles.

**Remedy:** curated micro-sets first; messy sorting later.

---

## Prototype-only teaching

Children recognize only extremely obvious examples.

**Remedy:** move quickly from prototypes to near-neighbours.

---

## Keyword classification

“Fixed means Repair.”
“Works means Efficacy.”

This can produce high apparent scores with no structural understanding.

**Remedy:** lexical traps, paraphrases, cross-domain examples and mandatory explanation.

---

## Treating the three questions as an assembly algorithm

Children may conclude that a cell means simply:

**Transformation + Topology + Persistence**

as three independently attached features.

That contradicts the supplied system, where the dimensions mutually constrain the unified cell meaning.

**Remedy:** call the questions an **address-finding procedure**, then repeatedly study whole-cell families.

---

## Confusing a coordinate with understanding a cell

Knowing “Repair × Focal × Bearing” is not enough.

**Remedy:** every cell needs prototype examples, rivals and explanation of its characteristic unified form.

---

## Too many cards

Volume can masquerade as rigor while overwhelming comparison.

**Remedy:** fewer, carefully selected contrasts.

---

## Badly chosen minimal pairs

A supposed one-dimensional contrast may secretly differ in two or three dimensions.

**Remedy:** expert validation of every anchor contrast before classroom use.

---

## Hiding all ambiguity

Children learn a false model in which language is always uniquely determined.

**Remedy:** use clear cases first, then carefully introduce ambiguity.

---

## Introducing ambiguity too early

Before boundaries are stable, ambiguity simply looks like arbitrariness.

**Remedy:** ambiguous cases belong after initial map competence.

---

## Unequal corpus quality across cells

Some positions may have many clear exemplars while others contain unstable families.

This is a property requiring empirical audit, not something curriculum design should conceal.

**Remedy:** give every cell a confidence status during corpus preparation:

* anchor-ready;
* usable;
* advanced/ambiguous;
* unresolved.

---

## Assessing with memorized training sentences

This measures recall of examples rather than structural transfer.

**Remedy:** reserve unseen corpus material for assessment.

---

## Teacher over-explanation

A teacher can unintentionally do all the discrimination for the child.

**Remedy:** ask contrast questions before explaining.

---

## False precision in the six Transformation definitions

The present brief does not supply sufficient formal definitions to guarantee that simplified classroom glosses preserve every intended boundary.

**Remedy:** perform a dedicated corpus/theory validation pass before freezing the teacher language.

This is probably the most important unresolved content-design issue before V1.

---

# 12. Final Recommended Prototype

Version 1 should be deliberately small.

Do **not** begin by building software or converting all 1,300 utterances into cards.

Build one flat repository/document and a small printable deck.

## A. Core repository document

Create these sections:

### 1. Theory reference

One page containing:

* three dimensions;
* 12 dimension values;
* 6×3×3 structure;
* explicit warning that coordinates constrain a unified cell rather than mechanically composing it.

### 2. Teacher's three questions

One page containing:

* What kind of change happened?
* What made it count as complete?
* How does the finished result remain?

Plus diagnostic prompts underneath each.

### 3. Fifteen-term teaching glossary

Use the formulations above only after they have been validated against the corpus and formal theory.

### 4. Six 3×3 maps

One map per Transformation Pattern.

Each cell should eventually contain:

* coordinate;
* one short whole-cell gloss;
* one anchor example;
* one nearest rival;
* one sentence explaining the distinction.

### 5. Contrast-set library

Prioritize approximately **18–24 highly controlled contrast sets** rather than hundreds of individual examples.

Each set records:

* target distinction;
* dimensions held stable;
* examples;
* expected classification;
* nearest rival;
* discriminating evidence;
* ambiguity notes.

### 6. Teaching script

The six-session sequence in this document.

### 7. Assessment pack

Keep all assessment examples separate from teaching examples.

### 8. Corpus-confidence notes

Flag structurally unstable cells instead of forcing them into clean instructional stories.

---

## B. Printable Version 1 card deck

Start with approximately **72 cards**, not 1,300.

A useful distribution is:

* **24 anchor cards** — very clear prototypes;
* **24 contrast cards** — chosen to create near-neighbour comparisons;
* **12 lexical-trap cards** — similar wording, different structures;
* **12 transfer cards** — held back from instruction.

The numbers can change after pilot testing. The design principle matters more than exact arithmetic.

Every cell does **not** need equal representation in the first teaching deck if some cells lack sufficiently clean examples.

A weak example should never be included merely to achieve numerical symmetry.

---

## C. The first corpus-selection task

Before classroom production, create a table for all 54 cells and ask expert annotators to nominate:

**Best prototype**
The clearest ordinary example.

**Best neighbour contrast**
An example that makes one local boundary visible.

**Best paraphrase pair**
Different wording, same structure.

**Best lexical trap**
Similar wording, different structure.

**Ambiguity candidate**
A case worth discussing later.

This turns *Pixels of Clarity* from a large archive into an instructional resource.

The existing corpus is particularly valuable because it contains convergence, variation and potentially informative disagreement.

---

## D. What to build first

The first tangible prototype should therefore be:

**1 teacher guide + 6 printable 3×3 boards + approximately 72 sentence cards + 1 short unseen assessment.**

Do not build the digital interface yet.

Run the physical version first.

Watch for five things:

1. Where children spontaneously invent the intended distinctions.
2. Which formal names they can attach easily after discovery.
3. Where they revert to keyword matching.
4. Which neighbouring cells repeatedly collapse together.
5. Which corpus items produce principled disagreement.

Those observations should determine the design of the eventual computer interface.

The computer should solve a problem children have already experienced—keeping track of a rich 54-position space—rather than introducing complexity before they know why the map exists. This preserves the strongest feature of the initial physical-to-digital idea in the brief.

---

# Final design principle

A child should never be asked to memorize a distinction that could first be **made visible through a comparison**.

The 54-cell architecture should arrive as the satisfying answer to a problem the child now understands:

> “We keep finding the same kinds of difference inside different kinds of completed events. How can we keep track of all of them?”

At that point the grid is not an arbitrary taxonomy.

It is a map of distinctions the child has already learned to see.

That is the shortest plausible route from ordinary sentences to the outcome the brief specifies: a child who can recognize a structure, locate it, defend the placement against its neighbours and transfer the distinction to new language.
# Pixels of Clarity: Second-Stage Learning-Design Report

## Executive judgment

The learner after Chapter 7 is at a genuine transition point.

The first seven chapters have not merely given the child some categories. They have trained a **new stance toward ordinary experience**: instead of asking only what something is about, the child has begun asking what kind of completion occurred, what made it enough, and what remains as a result. The formal teaching sequence has also deliberately made each new distinction necessary by allowing the previous sorting strategy to fail.

That success creates philosophical pressure. The child can now reasonably wonder why the same structural distinctions survive changes of subject matter, whether the patterns belong to sentences or to what the sentences make intelligible, whether they were invented or discovered, whether every intelligible thing gets a coordinate, and whether intelligibility guarantees truth. The project itself correctly recognizes that such questions may be evidence that the pedagogy is working rather than distractions from it.

My recommendation is therefore:

> **Finish the live third distinction first. Then bend.**

Chapter 8 should name and stabilize **Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy**. Interrupting before those names arrive would leave a distinction that Chapter 7 has deliberately brought to conceptual readiness suspended at exactly the wrong moment.

But Chapter 9 should **not** proceed directly to “Three Questions, One Thing.”

Instead, Chapter 9 should be the book's first explicit philosophical investigation:

> **Where are these patterns?**

It should not answer that question.

It should make the question investigable.

Then Chapter 10 should return to formal architecture with **Dimensional Conjunction and the 54-position reveal**. The philosophical bend will have changed the significance of the map: the child will no longer see 54 boxes merely as a classification system for sentence strips, but as a compact representation of structures whose recurrence they have begun to notice across language and life.

The optimal architecture from this point is:

> **linear prerequisite spine + recursive inquiry field**

—not a genuinely branching textbook.

The remaining book can probably be compressed from the proposed 18 chapters to **16 total chapters** without losing any invariant content.

# 1. Diagnosis After Chapter 7

## The learner in Chapter 1

The Chapter 1 child sees:

* sentence strips;
* similarities;
* topics;
* possible piles.

The first epistemic achievement is modest but profound:

> A pile is a claim about what is the same.

By Chapter 2, competing piles have shown that organizing material is already a form of reasoning. The child has begun distinguishing what a sentence is *about* from what *happened structurally*.

That distinction is the seed of everything that follows.

---

## The learner after Chapter 7

The Chapter 7 child can now see at least five things the Chapter 1 child could not.

### 1. Surface subject matter does not determine structural form

A machine, argument, route, team, distinction or dispute may differ dramatically in content while still being examinable through recurring structural questions.

The child has therefore acquired the beginnings of **structural invariance detection**.

That is more important than knowing category names.

---

### 2. “What happened?” has become multidimensional

The child no longer treats completion as one undifferentiated idea.

They can distinguish:

**Transformation Pattern**

> What kind of change happened?

from:

**Completion Topology**

> What made that change enough?

And Chapter 7 has now created a third unresolved distinction concerning the way the achieved result remains.
This means that the learner has moved from classification into something closer to **analysis**.

---

### 3. The child understands that similarity has levels

Two examples can be:

* about the same topic but structurally different;
* structurally similar despite different topics;
* identical on one dimension and different on another;
* near neighbours rather than simply “same” or “different.”

That is a substantial conceptual change.

The child's question is no longer merely:

> “Which pile?”

It is increasingly:

> “Same in what respect?”

---

### 4. Formal vocabulary has acquired a particular status

Because the book has consistently withheld names until distinctions have become perceptible, terms such as **Differentiation**, **Release**, and eventually **Standing** are experienced as *names for something seen*, not as definitions to be believed.

That is crucial.

The child has implicitly learned:

> The word is not the discovery.

This makes philosophical questioning much safer, because the child has already experienced the difference between a label and what the label tracks.

---

### 5. The child has acquired a method of inquiry

The deeper curriculum so far is not just Persistence.

It is:

* compare;
* notice;
* propose a distinction;
* test it;
* find a counterexample;
* refine the distinction;
* name it only when it earns its name.

The project explicitly describes the intended transfer of agency as:

**teacher asks → children ask one another → reader asks independently.**

By Chapter 7, therefore, the child is becoming capable of interrogating the grammar itself.

That is the conceptual pressure now accumulating.

---

## What pressure has accumulated?

Three kinds.

### Structural pressure

The child has three nearly complete dimensions but no picture yet of how they belong together.

They are ready to ask:

> “Are these three separate sorting tricks, or are they all ways of seeing one thing?”

That creates the need for **Dimensional Conjunction**.

### Explanatory pressure

The same distinctions seem to recur across unrelated situations.

The child is ready to ask:

> “Why does this work in so many places?”

### Boundary pressure

Once a system seems powerful, children naturally test where it breaks:

> “Does everything get one?”

> “What about something that isn't finished?”

> “What if two people disagree?”

> “What if it makes sense but isn't true?”

These are precisely the questions that should drive the later chapters rather than being answered prematurely.

---

# 2. The Questions Now Becoming Natural

These are ranked by how strongly they are generated by Chapters 1–7, not by their philosophical grandeur.

## 1. “Why do the same patterns keep appearing in completely different things?”

### Why it arises

The child has repeatedly watched topic change while structural questions continue to work.

A machine repair and an argument can share a structural distinction despite sharing almost no vocabulary.

That recurrence now requires explanation.

### Pedagogical status

**Investigate now; do not resolve metaphysically.**

This is the question that should produce the philosophical bend.

---

## 2. “Are we actually sorting sentences anymore?”

A sharper version is:

> “Is this something about the words, or about what the words let us notice?”

### Why it arises

Chapter 2 already distinguished **aboutness** from structural happening. Seven chapters later, that distinction has become powerful enough to turn back on the original activity.

The strips now look less like the object of study and more like evidence.

### Pedagogical status

**Investigate through paraphrases, retellings and nonverbal situations.**

Do not give an ontological answer.

---

## 3. “Where are these patterns?”

Possible child hypotheses include:

* in language;
* in people's understanding;
* in situations;
* in all of these somehow.

### Why it arises

Structural recurrence has survived dramatic changes in subject matter.

### Pedagogical status

**Keep open.**

The curriculum has not yet supplied machinery capable of deciding an ontology of the grammar.

This should be the central question of Chapter 9.

---

## 4. “Did we invent these patterns, or discover them?”

### Why it arises

The children did invent provisional labels and sorting arrangements, but some distinctions repeatedly survived testing and were later given formal names.

The pedagogical history itself therefore makes the invented/discovered contrast unavoidable.

### Pedagogical status

**Explicitly unresolved.**

The book should distinguish:

> “We invented these names.”

from the much larger question:

> “Did we invent the distinctions the names are tracking?”

The latter should remain open.

---

## 5. “Do all understandable things have one of these coordinates?”

### Why it arises

A successful grammar invites universal application.

Children test strong rules by looking for their limit.

### Natural bridge

This leads directly to:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

The invariant content explicitly requires this distinction.

### Pedagogical status

**Defer until after the map.**

The question becomes much more meaningful once the child knows what receiving a coordinate actually entails.

---

## 6. “What about something that hasn't become complete yet?”

### Why it arises

The entire curriculum so far concerns forms of completion. Eventually absence becomes conspicuous.

### Natural bridge

Again:

**no completion, no coordinate.**

But it can also provoke an important recognition:

Persistence is not being presented as a grammar of everything whatsoever.

It has a domain.

### Pedagogical status

**Clear answer later.**

---

## 7. “Can two people understand the same thing differently?”

### Why it arises

The children have already experienced disagreement about piles and have learned that different arrangements may answer different questions.

Now, with a more sophisticated grammar, they can ask whether disagreement means:

* somebody made a mistake;
* two completions are genuinely available;
* the expression is underspecified;
* different evidence is being privileged.

### Natural bridge

This leads toward:

* difficult cells;
* ambiguity;
* neighbour discrimination.

### Pedagogical status

**Investigate rather than settle globally.**

Specific cases may have clear answers. The general philosophical question need not.

---

## 8. “Can something make complete sense and still be wrong?”

### Why it arises

Once children become good at finding intelligible structure, they may implicitly equate successful structural interpretation with correctness.

The book must eventually break that inference.

The supplied plan already identifies this natural bridge from philosophical wondering into **intelligibility versus truth**.

### Pedagogical status

**Answer clearly, but later.**

Yes: intelligibility and truth must be distinguished.

But that lesson becomes strongest after children trust themselves as structural interpreters.

---

## 9. “Why haven't I noticed this before?”

### Why it arises

The child may feel that the system is simultaneously new and strangely familiar.

### Pedagogical status

**Discuss briefly; do not theorize excessively.**

A perfectly sufficient answer is:

> “Some things become easier to notice once we learn what differences to look for.”

The book does not need a philosophy of implicit cognition.

---

# 3. Should We Bend Now?

## Recommendation

**Yes—but one chapter later.**

Do **not** insert a separate philosophical interlude between Chapters 7 and 8.

Chapter 7 has deliberately generated a three-way distinction:

* achieved determination remaining;
* result constituted through relations;
* result remaining as capacity/effect/changed possibility.

The formal names **Standing, Bearing, Efficacy** have been withheld specifically so that recognition precedes terminology.

The child is therefore at the point of conceptual closure.

Interrupting now would violate the project's own learning rhythm:

> experience → comparison → discrimination → recognition → **naming**

The learner has reached recognition.

Give them the names.

Then bend.

---

## Why Chapter 9 is the right location

After Chapter 8, the child possesses the complete primitive dimensional vocabulary:

### Transformation Pattern

6 values.

### Completion Topology

3 values.

### Persistence Mode

3 values.

For the first time, the child can notice:

> “These same three kinds of question seem to keep working everywhere.”

That is the strongest possible moment for philosophical pressure.

A bend after Chapter 8 therefore does not interrupt a half-learned distinction.

It asks what the completed distinctions themselves mean.

---

## The shape of the bend

It should not be:

> “Here is a chapter where everybody sits around discussing the nature of reality.”

It should be an **investigation chapter**.

Concrete cases first.

Philosophical question second.

No forced resolution.

# 4. The Next Three Chapters

# Chapter 8 — Three Ways the Result Remains

## Opening situation

Return to a small number of Chapter 7 cases.

The children already agree on:

* Transformation Pattern;
* Completion Topology.

But they have been unable to explain the remaining contrast cleanly.

Put three child-generated provisional phrases on the board:

> **IT STAYS SETTLED**

> **IT HOLDS THROUGH HOW THINGS STAND TOGETHER**

> **IT CHANGES WHAT CAN HAPPEN**

Do not present these as definitions.

Ask whether the class can now find the same three-way difference in a second Transformation family.

---

## Central question

> **When the change is complete, how is the achieved result still available?**

---

## What the children do

They:

1. revisit a secure three-way contrast;
2. transfer the distinction into different subject matter;
3. find cases where surface vocabulary tempts them toward the wrong group;
4. explain the difference to one another;
5. finally receive formal names.

The names are:

* **Standing**
* **Bearing**
* **Efficacy**

---

## What the teacher does

Very little exposition.

The teacher asks:

> “What has to be true after the event is over?”

> “Does it simply stand settled?”

> “Does its achieved character depend on how something bears on something else?”

> “Does it remain as something that can now do, permit, govern, produce or alter something?”

Then:

> “You already know these three. Here are their names.”

---

## What is discovered

The third dimension becomes explicit and portable.

The child now possesses all twelve values of the three dimensions.

---

## What remains unresolved

A new question should be allowed to appear at the end:

> “Why have these three questions worked on so many completely different things?”

Do not answer.

That is the hook into Chapter 9.

---

## Formal Persistence content introduced

* Persistence Mode
* Standing
* Bearing
* Efficacy

---

## Why this chapter must occur here

Because Chapter 7 has already performed the experiential and discriminative work.

The concept is ready to be named.

Delaying the names now would no longer serve discovery.

It would merely postpone consolidation.

---

# Chapter 9 — Where Are These Patterns?

## Opening situation

Do not begin with a classroom discussion.

Begin with **one completed episode represented several ways**.

For example:

* a very short sentence;
* a longer paraphrase;
* a picture sequence;
* a tiny acted scene;
* somebody's retelling of what happened.

Ask:

> “Are we seeing the same structural completion in all four?”

Then present the reverse:

two similar sentences whose structures differ.

Now the old assumption—

> “We're sorting sentences”

—becomes unstable.

---

## Central question

> **Where is the thing we have been learning to notice?**

Possible hypotheses should come from the children:

* “in the words”;
* “in what happened”;
* “in how we understand what happened”;
* “maybe more than one of those.”

---

## What the children do

They conduct three small investigations.

### Investigation 1 — Change the words

Paraphrase a clear case while preserving the relevant completion as far as possible.

Ask:

> “Did the structure disappear when the wording changed?”

### Investigation 2 — Keep the words, change the situation

Give an expression whose interpretation changes with context.

Ask:

> “Did the sentence alone decide everything?”

### Investigation 3 — Remove the sentence

Show or enact a completed ordinary event.

Ask whether the structural questions are still usable.

The project explicitly intends recognition of Persistence structures in ordinary language **and life**, so this is a legitimate test of the learner's developing competence rather than a departure from the curriculum.

---

## What the teacher does

The teacher becomes an **inquiry engineer**.

They do not answer:

> “The patterns are in reality.”

They ask:

> “What does our evidence actually show?”

> “Which possibility does this example rule out?”

> “Which possibilities could still be true?”

> “What experiment would help us tell?”

When a child asks:

> “So which answer is right?”

the teacher can say:

> “We have evidence for some things. We do not yet have evidence that settles all of that question.”

This is not evasiveness.

It models intellectual calibration.

---

## What is discovered

Children should reach several modest conclusions:

* the exact vocabulary of a sentence is not the whole story;
* similar structural reasoning can recur across very different subject matter;
* changing representation does not necessarily destroy the distinction;
* some questions about the *status* of the grammar are larger than the classification tasks that first revealed it.

Nothing more metaphysical needs to be established.

---

## What remains unresolved

Deliberately preserve:

> Are the patterns discovered or invented?

> Are they in language, thought, situations, or some relation among them?

> Why do they recur so widely?

These become **long questions of the book**.

---

## Formal Persistence content introduced

No new primitive term.

That is important.

This chapter deepens the epistemic status of what has already been learned rather than adding another label.

It also broadens transfer from strips toward situations.

---

## Why this chapter must occur here

Because this is the first point at which the child genuinely possesses enough of the grammar for the philosophical question to be earned.

Earlier it would be atmosphere.

Here it is a consequence.

Later it would arrive after the taxonomy and risk feeling bolted on.

---

# Chapter 10 — Three Questions, One Completion

## Opening situation

The children now have three question cards:

1. What kind of change happened?
2. What made that change enough?
3. How does the achieved result remain?

Someone treats them like three independent stickers.

For example:

> “We just choose one from this pile, one from this pile and one from this pile.”

The teacher does not immediately correct them.

Instead, the class examines cases.

---

## Central question

> **Are the three answers separate facts, or are they three ways of locating one completed structure?**

---

## What the children do

They take several strong examples and repeatedly move between:

**whole case → three discriminations → whole case again**

The crucial movement is back.

After identifying the three coordinates, children must say:

> “Now tell me what the whole completed structure is.”

The address is never allowed to replace the thing addressed.

---

## What the teacher does

The teacher introduces the formal idea of **Dimensional Conjunction** only after the children have experienced why three independent labels are insufficient.

The teacher emphasizes:

> The questions help us navigate.

> The coordinate helps us locate.

> But the completion is one structure.

---

## What is discovered

The three dimensions belong together within a single completed intelligibility.

This is the conceptual bridge from **three learned distinctions** to **one grammar**.

---

## The 54-box reveal

Only now.

Put the six Transformation Patterns down first.

For each one:

* three Completion Topologies;
* three Persistence Modes.

Ask children to calculate the possible addresses:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54.**

Then reveal the complete structure.

The source explicitly requires the final learner to possess the 54-position grammar while preserving the distinction among the three dimensions and Dimensional Conjunction.

The emotional effect should be:

> “Oh. That's what we've been building.”

not:

> “Here are fifty-four things to memorize.”

---

## What remains unresolved

The map immediately generates new pressure:

> “Does every one of these boxes really occur?”

> “Does everything completed have exactly one?”

> “What if a case seems halfway between two?”

> “What about something that isn't completed at all?”

Excellent.

Those questions now drive the remainder.

---

## Formal Persistence content introduced

* Dimensional Conjunction
* complete 6 × 3 × 3 architecture
* the 54-position map
* coordinate navigation

---

## Why this chapter must occur here

The map should be the **compression of ten chapters of discovery**.

The philosophical bend of Chapter 9 means that its significance is now larger than a sorting board, while Dimensional Conjunction prevents it from becoming a mechanical combinatorics exercise.

This is the right moment for the reveal.

## 11. The Neighbour Test

The central competence becomes:

> **Why this box rather than the one beside it?**

This chapter absorbs the old “Move One Dial” idea but strengthens it.

Children work on:

* same Transformation, different Topology;
* same Transformation and Topology, different Persistence;
* closely related Transformation Patterns.

Tasks include:

> “Change one feature so it moves exactly one neighbour.”

> “Which square almost fits?”

> “What evidence rules that square out?”

This is where the 54-map becomes usable rather than merely impressive.

---

## 12. When the Boxes Push Back

This combines much of:

* Every Box Can Speak;
* The Hard Boxes.

Start with the expectation that a coordinate system should make everything easy.

Then let it fail.

Introduce:

* difficult cells;
* unstable examples;
* genuine ambiguity;
* disagreements caused by different interpretations.

Central question:

> **Is the problem in our reasoning, in the example, or in the fit between them?**

This is also the natural chapter for:

> “Can two people understand the same thing differently?”

Do not force every case into consensus.

---

## 13. Sometimes There Is No Coordinate

Begin with children trying too hard to classify everything.

Give them:

* fragments;
* descriptions with no achieved completion;
* situations still unfolding;
* cases where the relevant completion has not yet occurred.

Eventually someone should protest:

> “Maybe this doesn't have a box.”

Now formalize:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

This is essential because it makes the grammar's domain more precise rather than less powerful.

---

## 14. Clear Does Not Mean True

Begin with a beautifully intelligible falsehood.

Not a confusing falsehood.

Something whose structure is easy to understand and whose factual content is wrong.

Ask:

> “Did we fail to understand it?”

No.

Then:

> “So is understanding something the same as it being true?”

No.

Teach the invariant distinction between:

* intelligibility;
* truth.

The source explicitly identifies both **no completion/no coordinate** and **intelligibility versus truth** as terminal commitments.

This chapter pays off one of the philosophical questions instead of merely accumulating wonder forever.

Now sentence strips largely disappear.

Children look for structures in:

* conversation;
* instructions;
* stories;
* school activities;
* games;
* explanations;
* plans;
* repaired objects;
* disagreements;
* completed tasks.

The question becomes:

> **Can you notice the grammar when nobody has prepared an example for you?**

This is genuine transfer.

The teacher is now mostly absent as classifier.

## 16. Make the Map Speak

Final synthesis and generation.

Children must:

* generate a case for a coordinate;
* generate a neighbouring case;
* explain the one structural difference;
* diagnose a deliberately ambiguous case;
* identify a no-coordinate case;
* distinguish intelligibility from truth;
* reconstruct the three dimensions from memory.

Then return to the long questions from Chapter 9.

Not to answer them.

> “What do you now think?”

> “What evidence changed your mind?”

> “Which question became harder?”

> “Which question can you now ask more precisely?”

The final measure of intellectual growth is not that every large question has vanished.

It is that the child can ask better versions of them.

# 6. Philosophy Without Indoctrination

The book should adopt explicit rules.

## Rule 1 — Separate what the investigation shows from what somebody thinks it means

Use formulations such as:

> “Our examples show…”

and distinguish them from:

> “One possibility is…”

Never smuggle metaphysics into the first phrase.

---

## Rule 2 — Never convert recurrence into universality

The child may discover:

> “We keep finding these structures in very different places.”

That does **not** license:

> “Everything in reality has this structure.”

The source itself warns against premature claims of universality and specifically asks that the book not announce that the patterns are fundamental to reality.

The correct pedagogical response is:

> “That is a reason to investigate further.”

---

## Rule 3 — Give determinate answers when the theory genuinely supplies them

Open inquiry does not mean everything is open.

Examples:

**Does every sentence get a coordinate?**

No. The project explicitly preserves **no completion, no coordinate**.

**Is intelligibility identical to truth?**

No.

**Are Transformation Pattern, Completion Topology and Persistence Mode the same dimension?**

No.

**Are the dimensions merely three unrelated labels?**

No; Dimensional Conjunction must be taught.

The book must not fake uncertainty about its own invariant content.

---

## Rule 4 — Keep larger ontological claims larger

Questions such as:

> “Are these structures in reality?”

> “Were they discovered or invented?”

should not receive definitive answers merely because the curriculum has examples.

The current learning design does not establish such claims.

Say so.

---

## Rule 5 — Use examples to discipline philosophy

When a discussion starts floating upward, return to a case.

Ask:

> “What example made you think that?”

> “Can you find one that causes trouble for your idea?”

> “Would your claim still work here?”

This keeps philosophy continuous with the book's established method.

---

## Rule 6 — Children must be allowed to change level

A child may move among:

* classification question;
* language question;
* knowledge question;
* metaphysical question.

The teacher's task is to identify which level is currently being discussed.

For example:

> “Are you asking whether this sentence belongs in that cell, or whether the cells exist independently of us?”

Those are radically different questions.

Simply distinguishing them is philosophically educational.

---

## Rule 7 — “We don't know from this” is a legitimate conclusion

This phrase should become part of the culture of the book:

> **Our evidence does not settle that.**

It teaches children that intellectual honesty is compatible with strong inquiry.

---

# 7. Inquiry Devices

The book should not become structurally branching.

It needs prerequisite order.

But it should develop a **persistent layer of questions** running across the linear spine.

I recommend four recurring devices only.

## 1. Keep This Question

Used for questions that matter but cannot yet be settled.

For example:

> **KEEP THIS QUESTION**
> Are we inventing these patterns or discovering them?

Later chapters may return to it.

The purpose is memory across conceptual time.

---

## 2. Test It Somewhere Else

A tiny transfer challenge.

Example:

> **TEST IT SOMEWHERE ELSE**
> Find a completion today that does not involve a school sentence. Can the three questions still help?

Purpose:

prevent the grammar becoming associated only with book exercises.

---

## 3. What Would Change Your Mind?

Use sparingly for philosophical claims.

Example:

> “Maya thinks these patterns are only properties of sentences. What could she observe that would make her reconsider?”

Purpose:

turn opinions into testable positions.

---

## 4. Not Enough Yet

This should replace the more generic “A Question We Cannot Answer Yet.”

Example:

> **NOT ENOUGH YET**
> We have seen the same distinction in many settings. Is that enough to prove it exists everywhere?

Purpose:

teach the relationship between evidence and conclusion.

---

## Why not true branching?

Because the remaining formal material has real prerequisites.

Children should not reach:

* neighbour discrimination before the map;
* no-coordinate cases before coordinate meaning is secure;
* difficult ambiguity before clear classification competence;
* intelligibility versus truth before they can recognize intelligibility reliably.

The book should therefore have:

> **linear learning, nonlinear thinking.**

That is the right amount of nonlinearity.

# 9. Risks

## Risk 1 — The book becomes preachy

The moment the narrator says:

> “You have discovered the hidden architecture of reality,”

the inquiry collapses into doctrine.

### Protection

Use evidential language and leave ontological questions open.

---

## Risk 2 — Wonder becomes vagueness

A chapter full of:

> “What if everything is connected?”

would betray the precision established so far.

### Protection

Every philosophical question must arise from a particular discrimination, example or anomaly.

No atmospheric profundity.

---

## Risk 3 — Philosophy arrives before children possess enough material

This is why the bend should occur after Chapter 8, not Chapter 7.

The child should enter Chapter 9 possessing the complete primitive dimensional vocabulary.

---

## Risk 4 — Philosophy interrupts structural fluency

The philosophical bend should be **one chapter**, followed immediately by Dimensional Conjunction and the map.

Thereafter inquiry devices recur briefly inside formal chapters.

Do not alternate formal lesson / philosophy lesson for the remainder of the book.

---

## Risk 5 — The teacher becomes a faux-Socratic oracle

A common failure is:

Teacher: “But what *is* truth?”
Child: “I don't know.”
Teacher: “Interesting…”

That is not inquiry.

### Protection

Teacher questions should perform identifiable work:

* request evidence;
* distinguish claims;
* introduce countercases;
* identify what is unresolved;
* design a test.

---

## Risk 6 — Every child's opinion is treated as equally adequate

Philosophy for children does not mean:

> “There are no wrong answers.”

Some arguments are better supported than others.

Some classifications are wrong.

Some claims contradict the case.

### Protection

Preserve the book's existing norm:

> **What made you put those together?**

Reason-giving remains mandatory.

---

## Risk 7 — Every question receives an answer

This would turn wonder back into curriculum content to memorize.

### Protection

Maintain different statuses:

**settled by the grammar**
**supported by current evidence**
**open to investigation**
**not answerable yet**
**deliberately left open**

Children should know which status a question currently has.

---

## Risk 8 — No question ever receives an answer

The opposite failure is equally serious.

If everything becomes:

> “What do you think?”

the formal grammar loses authority and precision.

### Protection

Teach the invariant content decisively where it is invariant.

Open inquiry surrounds the theory; it does not dissolve it.

---

## Risk 9 — Premature universality

Repeated success may tempt both author and learner toward:

> “This system explains everything.”

The brief explicitly warns against this.

### Protection

Chapter 13 is especially important because **no completion, no coordinate** teaches that a powerful grammar can have boundaries.

---

## Risk 10 — The map becomes the climax

The 54-cell reveal is visually satisfying, so authors may unconsciously treat Chapter 10 as the destination.

It is not.

The terminal competence is active: notice, identify, locate, discriminate and explain.

The second half of the book must therefore be about **using and testing the map**, not admiring it.

---

# 10. Recommendation

The proposition:

> **“The book is not primarily a linear document. It is a document that progressively changes what the reader is capable of wondering about.”**

is substantially right, but needs one qualification.

As a theory of **what the book should do to the reader**, it is excellent.

As a theory of **how the book itself should be organized**, it is too strong.

Persistence still has prerequisites.

The optimal design is:

> **A linear sequence whose success is measured partly by the increasingly powerful nonlinear questions it makes possible.**

The chapters remain ordered.

The questions escape the chapter boundaries.

---

# What should we write next?

Write **Chapter 8: Three Ways the Result Remains**.

Do not insert the philosophical interlude yet.

Chapter 7 has already done the discovery work. The child has felt the distinction but does not possess its names. Complete that learning cycle by introducing:

* **Standing**
* **Bearing**
* **Efficacy**

The final pages of Chapter 8 should then change tone.

Do not preview the 54 boxes.

Instead, let a child notice something like:

> “Wait. We did this with fixing things. But we also did it with openings and arguments and all the other kinds. Why do the same questions keep working?”

Another child might ask:

> “Are these patterns in the sentences?”

Someone else:

> “But we found them in what happened too.”

The teacher should **not answer**.

The chapter should end on the first question that the book is deliberately unable to close:

> **Where are the patterns we have been finding?**

That becomes Chapter 9.

And Chapter 9 should be written not as a philosophical lecture but as an experiment the children perform on the status of their own discoveries.

Then Chapter 10 returns with:

> **Three Questions, One Completion**

and the 54-position map finally becomes visible.

That sequence—**name → wonder → integrate → reveal**—is the strongest continuation of what the first seven chapters have actually made the learner capable of doing.

The book's next developmental achievement should therefore not be “more philosophy.”

It should be something more precise:

> **The child begins to realize that a powerful way of seeing creates questions about seeing itself.**

That is the bend worth making.
